# Phase 1: Paper Analysis — Figueiredo et al. (2025)

**Paper:** "Presence of gluten and soy derived excipients in medicinal products and their implications on allergen safety and labeling." Scientific Reports, 15, 10976.
**Authors:** Figueiredo, Auxtero, Brás, Casimiro, Costa — Egas Moniz CiiEM, Portugal.
**Published:** March 31, 2025. Open Access CC BY 4.0.

---

## Part A: Scientific Critical Thinking Assessment

### 1. Methodology Critique

**Study design:** Cross-sectional descriptive audit of regulatory labels (SmPCs) from Portugal's INFOMED database. 308 products: 108 analgesics/antipyretics (paracetamol), 85 NSAIDs (ibuprofen), 115 antiasthmatics/bronchodilators. Appropriate design for a labeling prevalence question.

**Critical distinction the paper blurs:** The study measures the presence of *excipient names that could derive from gluten sources* in product labeling. It does **not** measure the presence of actual gluten protein. Pre-gelatinized starch (the most common finding, n=24 in analgesics) is almost always derived from maize or potato, not wheat. The classification "non-gluten-free" applied to any product containing pre-gelatinized starch is precautionary, not empirical.

**What the data actually show (Table 3):** Zero products across all 308 contained wheat, rye, barley, semolina, bran, malt, glucose syrup, gelatinized starch, or oats. Every "gluten-related" finding comes from starch derivatives whose botanical source is unspecified in the SmPC. This is a labeling ambiguity finding, not a gluten presence finding.

**The excipient driving the numbers:** Pre-gelatinized starch (24 analgesics) and sodium carboxymethyl starch (22 analgesics, 3 NSAIDs) account for nearly all "hits." Both are highly processed and frequently derived from corn/potato. Xanthan gum (9 analgesics, 4 NSAIDs) is flagged precautionarily.

### 2. Bias Detection

- **Classification bias (most significant):** Binary "non-gluten-free" based on excipient name rather than confirmed botanical source systematically inflates prevalence. A maize-derived starch product is classified identically to a wheat-derived one.
- **Database reliance bias:** SmPCs are regulatory documents, not certificates of analysis. The study measures labeling inadequacy as much as allergen presence.
- **Selection bias in categories:** Analgesic tablets are known to use starch excipients; inhalers use different formulation technology. Comparing them and finding "significant differences" is nearly tautological.
- **Single institution:** All authors from Egas Moniz. No external funding or conflicts declared.

### 3. Statistical Analysis Evaluation

**Tests used:** Pearson's chi-squared and Fisher's exact — appropriate for categorical data.

**The p < 0.001 finding:** Statistically valid but scientifically unremarkable. Different dosage forms use different excipients by pharmaceutical design. The comparison is between fundamentally different formulation technologies (tablets vs. inhalers).

**What is missing:**
- No confidence intervals. For 85 NSAIDs with 8.2% prevalence, the 95% CI is approximately 3.2–17.0%.
- No effect size measures (Cramér's V or phi).
- No dose-level analysis. Excipient quantity per dose matters enormously for clinical risk.
- No temporal analysis.
- The non-significant generic/branded result for gluten may reflect insufficient power, not true equivalence.

### 4. Evidence Quality Assessment

| Domain | Rating | Rationale |
|---|---|---|
| Risk of bias | Moderate-High | Classification inflates prevalence |
| Inconsistency | N/A | Single study |
| Indirectness | High | Excipient names used as proxy for allergen presence |
| Imprecision | Moderate | No confidence intervals; small subgroups |
| Publication bias | Low | Descriptive study |
| **Overall — "products contain allergens"** | **Low** | Not demonstrated |
| **Overall — "labeling doesn't exclude allergen sources"** | **Moderate** | Well-supported |

### 5. Claim Evaluation

**What the data support:**
- Starch-derived excipients of unspecified botanical origin are common in paracetamol tablets (51.2% of solid oral forms). **Supported.**
- These *could theoretically* derive from gluten-containing grains. **Supported as possibility, not demonstrated reality.**
- SmPC labeling does not routinely specify botanical source of starch excipients. **Strongly supported — most defensible finding.**
- Antiasthmatics (inhaled) contain 0% of these excipients. **Supported, unsurprising.**

**Overreach:** The title claims "implications on allergen safety" but provides no clinical, immunological, or analytical chemistry evidence of actual allergen presence or adverse reactions.

### 6. Logical Issues

- **Equivocation:** "Non-gluten-free" conflates "label cannot confirm gluten-free" with "contains gluten."
- **Uncalibrated precaution:** No attempt to estimate what proportion of starch excipients are actually wheat-derived.
- **Ecological fallacy risk:** 44.4% of products ≠ 44.4% of patients exposed. Market share data could estimate patient-level exposure but isn't used this way.

## Part C: Key Verdicts for This Project

### Q1: Is this study solid enough to anchor a journalism piece?

**Yes, but the framing must be precise.** The study is published in a legitimate Nature portfolio journal, uses a transparent methodology, and addresses a real regulatory gap. However, the headline numbers (44.4%) overstate actual risk.

**The story it supports:** "Patients with celiac disease cannot reliably determine from drug labels whether their medications contain gluten-source excipients, because starch sourcing is not consistently disclosed."

**The story it does NOT support:** "Nearly half of common painkillers contain hidden gluten."

### Q2: What are the honest limitations to disclose to readers?

1. The 44.4% figure is a worst-case ceiling, not a measured prevalence of actual gluten. Most starch excipients are likely corn/potato-derived.
2. Zero products contained any explicitly wheat-derived excipient — every "hit" comes from starch derivatives with unspecified source.
3. This is a Portuguese study. US formulations, excipient choices, and labeling requirements differ.
4. No patients were harmed and no adverse reactions were documented. Clinical significance is theoretical.
5. The study is label-based only — actual gluten levels were not measured.
6. Published in Scientific Reports (broad-scope megajournal), not replicated or externally validated.

### Q3: Is US replication viable with DailyMed?

**Yes — and a US replication would actually be stronger.** DailyMed advantages:
- **Structured XML data** with machine-readable `<inactiveIngredient>` sections enabling automated extraction
- **Scale:** 140,000+ products vs. 308 — far larger samples, better statistical power
- **Excipient specificity:** Some US labels specify "pregelatinized maize starch" rather than just "pregelatinized starch," allowing disambiguation
- **Public API access** for systematic extraction
- **Regulatory gap angle:** FALCPA requires wheat declaration in *foods* but not *drugs* — newsworthy in itself

**Key differences to account for:**
- US has different excipient disclosure requirements than EU
- Generic formulations may use different excipients than Portuguese equivalents
- Many US OTC analgesics are marketed under OTC monographs with less standardized labeling
- The excipient classification framework needs validation against FDA Inactive Ingredient Database naming

This assumption is based on Table 3 showing zero confirmed gluten grains and all "hits" coming from starch derivatives. > ⚠️ **ASSUMPTION:** Pre-gelatinized starch and sodium carboxymethyl starch in pharmaceutical products are predominantly corn/potato-derived. This is consistent with industry practice but is not verified for the specific Portuguese products in this study. The original authors did not investigate source.
